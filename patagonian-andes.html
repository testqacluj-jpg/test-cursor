The Andes are the longest continental mountain range in the world, stretching 8,900 kilometres along the entire western spine of South America. They run through seven countries — Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia, Chile, and Argentina — and reach their wild, dramatic southern conclusion in Patagonia.

In Patagonia, the Andes do not simply end — they shatter. Numerous transverse and longitudinal depressions cut through this rugged portion of the chain, and many ranges are occupied by vast ice fields, glaciers, rivers, lakes, and fjords. Here the mountains are not merely tall — they are untamed.

01

Geography & Formation

The Andean mountain system is the result of global plate-tectonic forces during the Cenozoic Era — roughly the past 65 million years — built upon even earlier geologic activity. About 250 million years ago, the crustal plates of Earth's landmass were joined into the supercontinent Pangaea. The subsequent breakup of Pangaea, and of its southern portion, Gondwana, dispersed the plates outward.

The collision of two of these plates — the continental South American Plate and the oceanic Nazca Plate — gave rise to the mountain-building activity that produced the Andes. Tectonic forces above the subduction zone along the entire west coast of South America continue to shape the range to this day, making it one of the most geologically active mountain systems on Earth.

The Patagonian Andes rise north of the Strait of Magellan, forming the westernmost wall of the region. On the Argentine side, Patagonia is divided into Andean, Central, and Atlantic zones. Andean Patagonia is the most mountainous, where the mighty Andean range crosses. Central Patagonia is dominated by vast plains; Atlantic Patagonia forms the coastal fringe.

The Patagonian tableland rises in a series of terraces from high coastal cliffs to the foot of the Andes, climbing from about 90 metres at the coast to 3,000 feet at the base of the mountains. South of the Río Negro, the plains grow more irregular, marked by volcanic eruptions and basaltic sheets from the geologically recent past.

Ice Fields & Glaciers

"Patagonia is home to the Northern and Southern Icefields — the second largest continental ice fields in the world, after those found in Antarctica."

Along the Chilean side of the Patagonian Andes lie the Northern and Southern Icefields, two of the most spectacular concentrations of ice on Earth outside the poles. The Northern Icefield covers a total surface of about 4,200 square kilometres and extends for almost 200 kilometres. The larger Southern Icefield covers around 14,000 square kilometres and stretches over 350 kilometres in length.

These vast reservoirs of ice feed nearly 100 glaciers across the region. The most famous, the Perito Moreno glacier in Los Glaciares National Park, calves spectacularly into the turquoise waters of Lago Argentino. Visitors can hike across its surface, watch icebergs crash into the water, or approach by boat — an encounter with a living, moving landscape on a scale difficult to comprehend.

Yet this frozen world is shrinking. Glaciers have retreated in all Andean countries over the last four decades due to atmospheric warming. An extensive survey concluded that current glacier retreat is unprecedented since at least the Little Ice Age of the mid-17th century. The loss is most pronounced on small glaciers at low altitudes — several have disappeared entirely, with more projected to follow.

The consequences extend far beyond the mountains. The Andes contribute up to 35 percent of annual river basin water supply in the more arid regions of Peru and Chile. Several major cities rely on glacial runoff for drinking water and hydroelectric power. The shrinking ice of Patagonia is thus not only a visual loss — it is a growing threat to the livelihoods of millions downstream.

03

Iconic Peaks & Places

🏔

Cerro Fitz Roy

Rising above El Chaltén in Los Glaciares National Park, Fitz Roy's jagged granite spire is one of the most photographed peaks in the world. The trek to Laguna de los Tres, at its base, is considered among the finest in Patagonia.

Elevation — 3,405 m / 11,171 ft
🗼

Cerro Torre

A needle of granite and ice, Cerro Torre was long considered the world's most difficult climb. Its near-vertical walls, draped in mushrooms of rime ice, form a silhouette unlike any other mountain on Earth.

Elevation — 3,128 m / 10,262 ft
🧊

Perito Moreno Glacier

One of very few glaciers in the world that is not retreating, the Perito Moreno advances at up to 2 metres per day. Its ice wall, rising 60–70 metres above the lake surface, periodically ruptures in dramatic collapses that draw crowds of awestruck spectators.

Area — 250 km² · Los Glaciares NP
🌊

Strait of Magellan

The natural passage separating continental South America from Tierra del Fuego marks the southern limit of the Patagonian Andes. First navigated by Ferdinand Magellan in 1520, this narrow waterway between mountain walls has shaped the history of global exploration.

Length — 570 km · Connects Pacific & Atlantic
🌲

Los Alerces National Park

A UNESCO World Heritage Site, Los Alerces protects over 260,000 hectares of forest, lakes, and rivers. Ancient larch trees — some more than 2,600 years old — grow here, making it among the most ecologically significant forests in South America.

Patagonian Andes, Argentina
🦅

Cordillera Darwin

Entirely within Chilean territory, Cordillera Darwin stretches from the Beagle Channel to Almirantazgo Fjord. Much of this range remains unexplored, accessible only by sea or expedition. Its remote, unvisited valleys represent one of the last true wilderness frontiers on Earth.

Tierra del Fuego, Chile

Climate & When to Visit

The Patagonian Andes shape their own weather, and it is famously capricious. The Andes act as a dramatic barrier between the wet Pacific and the arid steppe: moisture-laden westerly winds deposit enormous rainfall on the Chilean side, feeding lush temperate rainforests and feeding the great icefields, while the Argentine side lies in deep rain shadow — dry, windswept, and vast.

Winds in Patagonia are legendary. They can arrive without warning, gusting to extraordinary force even on otherwise clear days. Trekkers on the famous W-Circuit in Torres del Paine have learned to read the sky with particular attention. Yet on calm days, the clarity of the light here — far from industrial haze, at the bottom of the world — is unlike anywhere else on Earth.

The region spans two countries with different infrastructure and access. Argentine Patagonia is served by National Route 40, running north to south through the Andean mountains, connecting destinations like San Martín de los Andes, Bariloche, and El Chaltén. Chilean Patagonia is more rugged, with extensive waterways and ferry routes linking its fjords and archipelagoes.

Best Times to Visit

  • Nov – Mar Austral summer — ideal for trekking, glacier hikes, and camping. Long daylight hours. Busiest season; book accommodation well in advance.
  • Apr – May Autumn brings vivid foliage of lenga beech forests. Fewer crowds, cooler temperatures. Excellent for photography and visiting towns like El Chaltén and Ushuaia.
  • Jun – Aug Austral winter — ski season at resorts like Cerro Catedral near Bariloche. Snow transforms the mountains. Some hiking trails and park services are limited.
  • Sep – Oct Spring — wildflowers emerge, wildlife is active. Whale season begins at Península Valdés. Weather is transitional; expect rapidly changing conditions.
